# The Compliant Booth Model — LOCKED

> **This file is locked.** Do not edit, soften, or omit it from a packet. If an ambassador
> disagrees with a rule, explain the campus policy behind it and escalate to the Foundation.
> Every rule here was found in a real, written university policy — none of them are
> hypothetical caution.

## Why one model instead of per-campus rules

You could hand each ambassador a different set of rules for each campus. That fails in
practice: someone tables at three schools in a week and blends them. So the booth is
designed to the **strictest rule found anywhere**, and then it is compliant everywhere.

This is not a conservative reading. It is the literal intersection of four real policies:

- **Montana State (Catapalooza):** *"Vendors are prohibited from seeking a monetary
  exchange for products, services, or donations."* But the same rules expressly permit
  distributing *"informational items, coupons, hand-outs, freebies, company contact
  information"* and collecting contacts for post-event follow-up. **That permission is the
  shape of the whole model.**
- **Weber State (Wildcat Block Party):** *"Vendors may not have students sign any kind of
  contract for services on site; this must take place at the Vendor's place of business."*
- **Colorado State (Lory Student Center):** *"No financial transactions (including
  requesting credit card information, Venmo or similar app info, etc) are permitted on the
  Plaza."* Written broadly enough to reach wallet onboarding.
- **WSU (WAC 504-34-140):** distribution *"by means of accosting, confronting, detaining,
  or waylaying individuals or by hawking is prohibited."*

There is a second reason to build it this way, and it matters more than the first. The
CARD Act of 2009 banned issuers from offering a college student *"any tangible item to
induce such student to apply for or open an open-end consumer credit plan"* on campus.
That law does not reach DGD — there is no credit plan. But *"$21 in exchange for signing
up, at a table on campus"* is a near-perfect factual match to the conduct Congress banned.
**Administrators will react to the pattern, not the citation.** A booth that educates and
captures an email doesn't trigger the pattern. A booth that completes signups on the spot
does.

## The rules

### 1. Age-gate at 18, before anything else happens
Not 13. COPPA's under-13 threshold is the wrong statute and it will lull you — it covers
essentially no undergraduates. The real problem is contract law: a person under 18 can
generally disaffirm a contract, meaning a minor can take the credit, refer friends, and
then void the terms of service, the arbitration clause, and any clawback.

The trap is that **the `.edu` path is specifically the path that admits minors.**
Dual-enrolled high schoolers are on every campus in every state researched, and they
frequently hold institution-issued `.edu` addresses. Colorado's and Oregon's minors
provisions are keyed to *actual knowledge* — a rep who can see they're talking to a high
schooler creates knowledge the company then has.

### 2. Educate at the table. Transact nowhere near it.
Hand out material, answer questions, explain the protocol. Do not complete a signup, do
not accept terms, do not open a wallet, do not take a payment credential. This single
choice is what makes the booth legal at Montana State, Weber State and Colorado State
simultaneously — and at every campus stricter than they are.

### 3. Capture an email; finish validation off-campus and off-clock
The follow-up happens later, elsewhere, on the student's own time. This is the operational
translation of rule 2 and the thing most likely to be quietly skipped when a booth is busy.

### 4. Re-confirm interest after the event, and never build a list
Montana State again, verbatim: *"If you collect contact information from potential
customers interested in purchasing goods or services, you must contact the potential
customer after Catapalooza re-confirming their interest before any transactions take
place"* and *"do not add a potential customer to any mailing list without their specific
consent."* One follow-up that re-confirms interest. Then nothing unless they opt in.

### 5. No government-ID scanning at a folding table
A BSA/KYC obligation attaches only if DGD is a money services business. If it isn't, there
is no obligation compelling ID collection — and collecting ID documents is pure liability
with no offsetting compliance benefit, plus possible GLBA Safeguards Rule exposure. Don't
collect what you don't need.

### 6. No raffles, anywhere
Prohibited for recognized student organizations at Boise State under Idaho gambling law
(fines to $10,000), university-wide at the University of Arizona, and for ASU departments
per an Arizona Attorney General determination. This rules out any giveaway structured as
a raffle, however casual.

### 7. Ambassadors disclose the material connection every time
FTC Endorsement Guides, 16 C.F.R. Part 255. Student referrers are compensated endorsers,
and the disclosure obligation applies in a dorm-room conversation and an unlabeled
Instagram story, not just in an ad. **Liability for non-disclosure runs to the company.**
Make it a contract term with a clawback, supply pre-written disclosure copy, and spot-check
posts. Nineteen-year-olds paid per signup will not disclose consistently without it.

### 8. Never say "free money" or "risk-free." Never forecast value.
FTC Act §5 deception risk, and the fastest route to a campus complaint. Describing credit
as "free money" while it is locked, non-transferable or contingent is straightforwardly
deceptive.

### 9. Stay behind the table. Don't approach passers-by.
WSU bars distribution "by accosting, confronting, detaining, or waylaying individuals or by
hawking." Wyoming: *"All the members of your group must stay behind the table."* Weber:
no "aggressive sales tactics." Same rule three ways.

### 10. Bring the written approval to the table
Most policies make the person staffing the booth the one asked to leave. Arizona:
*"Unapproved vendors are prohibited on campus"* — approval status is verifiable by phone at
(520) 626-2630. Have the email or permit on the phone, not in someone's memory.

## Insurance requirements found so far

Check the campus record — these vary and several campuses don't publish theirs.

| Campus | Requirement |
|---|---|
| University of Oregon | $1M CGL / $2M aggregate, UO named as additional insured. TULIP available. |
| UNLV | ≥$1,000,000 combined single limits, Board of Regents as additional insured, plus hold-harmless/indemnification. |
| Colorado School of Mines | $1M per accident bodily injury; $3M per accident property damage. URMIA-TULIP available. |
| Western Washington | May require an advance deposit, bond, or insurance (POL-U1000.06). |
| University of Arizona | Certificate required **only** for vendors serving food/beverage or displaying a vehicle — DGD is likely exempt. |
| ASU | Events involving non-university individuals need ≥2 weeks' notice for added approvals, insurance and fees. |

TULIP (Tenant Users Liability Insurance Policy) is the usual vehicle for a one-day booth
and is cheap. Get it before you need it.

## The pre-flight, in one paragraph you can read aloud

> At the table we teach and we listen. We check that everyone we talk to is 18. We hand
> out material and we take an email if someone wants one — we do not open accounts, take
> payments, scan IDs, run raffles, or ask anyone to agree to anything on the spot. We stay
> behind the table and let people come to us. We say plainly that we're with Digital Gold
> and that referrers get credit. We never call it free money and we never talk about price.
> Everything else happens later, off campus, on their time.
