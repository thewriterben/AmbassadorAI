# ⚠⚠ PART 0 — THE STATE-LEVEL FINDING THAT OUTRANKS EVERY CAMPUS POLICY

## New York's BitLicense — 23 NYCRR Part 200, administered by NY DFS

**This is not a campus-access question. It is a threshold question about whether DGD may lawfully sign up a single New York resident at all.** New York is the only U.S. state with a bespoke, license-gated virtual-currency regime. Nothing in this packet matters if the answer here is no.

### The operative text

**23 NYCRR § 200.3(a) — the licence requirement:**
> "**No Person shall, without a license obtained from the superintendent as provided in this Part, engage in any Virtual Currency Business Activity.**"
- https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/23-NYCRR-200.3

**23 NYCRR § 200.2(q) — the definition:**
> "*Virtual Currency Business Activity* means the conduct of any one of the following types of activities **involving New York or a New York Resident**:"
> (1) "receiving Virtual Currency for Transmission or Transmitting Virtual Currency, except where the transaction is undertaken for non-financial purposes and does not involve the transfer of more than a nominal amount of Virtual Currency";
> (2) "storing, holding, or maintaining custody or control of Virtual Currency on behalf of others";
> (3) "buying and selling Virtual Currency as a customer business";
> (4) "performing Exchange Services as a customer business";
> (5) "**controlling, administering, or issuing a Virtual Currency**."
> …
> "The development and dissemination of software in and of itself does not constitute Virtual Currency Business Activity."
- https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-york/23-NYCRR-200.2
- DFS's own restatement: https://www.dfs.ny.gov/apps_and_licensing/virtual_currency_businesses

**23 NYCRR § 200.2(p) — "Virtual Currency" means:**
> "any type of digital unit that is used as a medium of exchange or a form of digitally stored value."
Excluded: (1) digital units usable only inside online gaming platforms with no external market and no conversion to fiat or other virtual currency; (2) "digital units redeemable only for goods/services within customer affinity programs, **not convertible to fiat or virtual currency**"; (3) digital units used as part of Prepaid Cards.

**23 NYCRR § 200.3(c) — the only two exemptions:**
> (1) "Persons that are chartered under the New York Banking Law and are approved by the superintendent to engage in Virtual Currency Business Activity";
> (2) "**merchants and consumers that utilize Virtual Currency solely for the purchase or sale of goods or services or for investment purposes.**"

### Applying it to DGD's model

DGD issues USD-denominated credit redeemable for a digital asset, with referral accrual. Run it through the five prongs:

- **§ 200.2(q)(5) — "controlling, administering, or issuing a Virtual Currency."** This is the prong that bites. If DGD is the issuer/administrator of the digital asset the credit redeems into, and the signup involves a New York resident, the activity is on its face inside the definition. There is no "small volume," "student," or "promotional" carve-out anywhere in Part 200. The trigger is *involving New York or a New York Resident* — a single student sign-up on a Manhattan quad is enough.
- **§ 200.2(q)(1) — transmitting.** Referral accrual that moves value between accounts on DGD's ledger is at least arguably Transmission. The "non-financial purposes and… nominal amount" carve-out is narrow and is drafted around things like notarisation, not marketing incentives.
- **§ 200.2(q)(2) — custody.** If DGD holds the accrued credit for the user pending redemption, that is "storing, holding, or maintaining custody or control of Virtual Currency on behalf of others."

**Does any exemption apply? Almost certainly not.**
- § 200.3(c)(1) — DGD is not a New York Banking Law charter.
- § 200.3(c)(2) — the merchant/consumer exemption covers people who *use* virtual currency to buy things or to invest. **DGD is on the issuer side of the transaction, not the merchant/consumer side.** This exemption does not launder an issuer.
- **The one argument worth putting to counsel** is the § 200.2(p)(2) *affinity-program* exclusion: a digital unit "redeemable only for goods/services within customer affinity programs, not convertible to fiat or virtual currency." If DGD's credit were locked — redeemable only inside DGD, never convertible — it might sit outside the definition of Virtual Currency entirely. **But the premise of the model is that the credit redeems into a digital asset, and if that asset is convertible, the exclusion fails on its own terms.** This is the single question to send to a New York-licensed lawyer, framed exactly this way.

### The money and the machinery
- BitLicense application fee: **$5,000**. NYDFS also has statutory authority (S.8008C, signed April 9, 2022, effective June 8, 2022) to levy annual assessments on licensed virtual-currency businesses "in amounts NYDFS' Superintendent deems 'just and reasonable.'" — https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/new-law-gives-nydfs-authority-to-make-virtual-currency-business-activities-in-new-york-more-expensive
- Applications are filed through NMLS. DFS's virtual-currency contact page obfuscates its email in the rendered HTML and **publishes no phone number for the virtual currency unit** — https://www.dfs.ny.gov/apps_and_licensing/virtual_currency_businesses
- § 200.3 also bars licensees from operating through unlicensed agents: "Each Licensee is prohibited from conducting any Virtual Currency Business Activity through an agent or agency arrangement when the agent is not a Licensee." **Read that as a direct warning about ambassador/affiliate structures.** An unlicensed campus ambassador collecting sign-ups for a licensee is exactly the arrangement the sentence forbids.

### What this means operationally
**Do not run a New York campus tour that collects sign-ups until counsel has cleared § 200.2(q)(5) in writing.** An information-only presence — literature, education, a club talk with no wallet creation, no credit issuance, no referral accrual — is a materially different legal posture from a sign-up table, and is the only posture that should be contemplated before that clearance exists. This finding is carried into the anchor campus's `policy_key` labelled **STATE REGULATORY note — HIGHEST PRIORITY**.

**This is research, not legal advice.** The value here is that the question is now precisely framed with the citation attached.

# PART 0B — THE TWO SYSTEMS, AND WHAT NEW YORK DOES NOT HAVE

## SUNY and CUNY are two separate systems. Do not conflate them.

**SUNY** — 64 campuses, its own Board of Trustees. Four campuses in this packet: Stony Brook, Buffalo, Binghamton, Albany.
**CUNY** — 25 campuses, its own Board of Trustees. Three campuses in this packet: Baruch, Hunter, City College.

They share nothing but the State of New York. Their facility-use rules are written independently and read differently.

### SUNY systemwide policy — three documents, all confirmed

**1. SUNY Policy 5607, "Commercial Use Policy (Use of University Facilities for Commercial Purposes)," effective March 28, 2012** — https://www.suny.edu/sunypp/documents.cfm?doc_id=704
Verbatim operative provisions:
> "Use of University facilities for instruction, research and public service take priority over the commercial use of University facilities."
> Commercial activity "shall not be in conflict with, and shall advance the mission of, the campus."
> Such use "shall not infringe upon, delay or conflict with the normal operation of the campus."
> Use "shall not have a significant potential for material adverse effect on the reputation of the campus for academic integrity and independence."
> "Commercial use shall not compete with or replicate activities of the campus auxiliary services corporation, campus foundation, or other campus-related entities."
> Use "shall not violate existing agreements between the campus…and vendors providing goods or services on campus."
> Use "shall conform to federal tax law restrictions on private use of facilities financed by tax-exempt bonds."

Crucially, **5607 devolves the actual rules**: each campus must adopt local policies specifying authorization procedures, eligible facilities, cost structures, and fair-market appraisal standards. So SUNY does *not* resolve four campuses at a stroke — it sets a reputational-risk screen and then sends you to the campus. The "material adverse effect on the reputation of the campus" clause is the one a risk-averse administrator will reach for when a crypto project asks to table.

**2. SUNY Policy 5603, "Use of Facilities by Non-Commercial Organizations," effective June 22, 2020** — https://www.suny.edu/sunypp/documents.cfm?doc_id=374
Covers not-for-profit, governmental, charitable, civic and religious groups. Explicitly excludes auxiliary services corporations, recognised student government organisations and alumni organisations from its terms. Key language:
> The University intends "not to compete with private business enterprises having similar facilities."
> "Auxiliary services, such as food, legal beverages, vending machines and bookstore, shall not be provided" to these organisations except incidentally.
> "Use does not infringe upon, delay or conflict with the normal operation of the University's campuses."
> Organisations "may charge an admission fee or accept donations subject to pertinent state and local laws and approval of the campus president."
**DGD is not a non-commercial organisation. 5603 is the wrong door and it is important not to walk through it by accident** — several SUNY campuses (UAlbany, Plattsburgh) point third-party free-speech applicants at 5603, which quietly confirms that the third-party forum route is for *non-commercial* expression.

**3. SUNY Rules for the Maintenance of Public Order, Document 3653, effective June 10, 2009** — https://www.suny.edu/sunypp/documents.cfm?doc_id=351
Adopted under Article 129-A of the Education Law (the "Henderson Rules"). The president must "inform any licensee or invitee who shall violate any provisions of these rules that his or her license or invitation is withdrawn" and direct them to leave; for non-affiliated violators, must "inform the violator that they are not authorized to remain on the property of the campus and direct them to leave the premises," with ejection and trespass/loitering prosecution to follow. Individuals need authorisation to use campus property or facilities. **This is the enforcement backstop — it is how a campus removes an unpermitted table.**

**A fourth, campus-level document quotes SUNY-wide language that matters more than any of the above.** Binghamton Policy 203, "Business and Commercial Activities on Campus" (last revised 10/20/2025), opens:
> "**It is the policy of the State University of New York (University) that no authorization will be given to private commercial enterprises to operate on University campuses**" — except for specified authorised services (food, beverages, bookstore, vending, banking, cultural events).
- https://www.binghamton.edu/operations/policies/policy-203.html
I could not locate this sentence's source document on suny.edu itself; searches on the exact string return only Binghamton. **Treat it as authoritative at Binghamton and as a strong indicator of SUNY posture elsewhere, but do not cite it as "SUNY policy" to another SUNY campus without asking them to name their own document.**

### CUNY systemwide policy

**CUNY Policy 4.02, "Facilities Use," approved by the Board of Trustees December 4, 2017 (amending a February 28, 2005 version)** — https://policy.cuny.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/6/page-assets/general-policy/Policy-4.02-Facilities-Use-approved-by-BOT-120417.pdf
This is the single most useful systemwide document in New York, because it *names commercial users as a category*. It establishes six priority categories:
> "(1) Host college departments… (2) Users affiliated with the host college… (3) Other CUNY colleges… (4) Government agencies and non-profit organizations… (5) Union organizations… **(6) All other users, including commercial users.**"
And then the operative discretion clause:
> "**Colleges shall permit use under categories 1 through 5 and may permit use under category 6**" — i.e. commercial use is discretionary, never mandatory.
Fees: "Fair market value must be charged for partisan political use"; commercial users are charged direct costs at standard rates; affiliated users pay no Use Fee. Insurance: non-affiliated users must "provide evidence of appropriate and adequate insurance protection (including liquor liability if alcohol will be served)." Co-sponsorship: if an affiliated user co-sponsors with outside organisations, "the host college may charge a Use Fee… apportioned to the outside organization(s)" — **note that this contemplates sponsorship rather than forbidding it; CUNY has no systemwide anti-fronting rule.**

**⚠ The primary CUNY facility-use policy PDF is ROBOTS-BLOCKED to research tooling:** https://www.cuny.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/page-assets/about/administration/offices/legal-affairs/policies-resources/Facility-Use-Policy.pdf returns ROBOTS_DISALLOWED. **CUNY Bylaws Article XV** (https://policy.cuny.edu/bylaws/article-xv/) is likewise robots-blocked. Policy 4.02 above was retrievable and is the operative document; the two blocked documents should be read by a human before any CUNY commitment.

**CUNY insurance dollar limits** come from John Jay's "Best Practices for Use of Campus Facilities by Student Organizations" (July 2024) — https://www.jjay.cuny.edu/sites/default/files/2024-07/BestPracticesforUseofCampusFacilitiesFINALwhighlight.pdf
> Low-risk events (e.g. poetry reading under 50 people): "$500,000 may be sufficient"
> Standard events (lectures, dance, music): "$1,000,000 (one million dollars) per occurrence and $2,000,000 (two million dollars) aggregate is usually adequate"
> High-risk (outdoor carnivals, sporting events): "$2,000,000 (two million dollars) to $5,000,000 (five million dollars) range may be appropriate"
> Insurance broker named in the document: "InterCity Agency, Inc. (718-279-7705)"
That document also states plainly: "These best practices are **not** intended to address facility use by external groups, such as community groups, not-for-profit organizations, and commercial entities that need space for meetings, conferences and events" — so it governs the *club*, not DGD, and the club route it describes is the one to use.

### ⚠ New York has NO campus free-speech statute.

Unlike Oklahoma (70 O.S. § 2120), Wisconsin, Arizona, Florida and roughly twenty other states, **New York has not enacted a campus free-expression statute applying to SUNY or CUNY.** SUNY's own 2021 legislative survey memo (https://system.suny.edu/sci/news/10-7-21-free-expression-legislation/index.html) catalogues statutes in seventeen-plus other states and **discusses no New York bill at all**. A2309 (2023) was introduced but I found no enactment. PEN America's tracker likewise shows New York without one (https://pen.org/campus-free-speech/the-law-and-campus-free-speech/).

What New York publics *do* have is Article 129-A of the Education Law (the Henderson Act), which requires every college — public and private — to adopt and file rules for the maintenance of public order. That statute is a *restriction* framework, not a speech-protective one. **Do not let an ambassador claim a New York campus free-speech statute. There isn't one.** SUNY and CUNY publics remain bound by the First Amendment as state actors, but commercial speech gets intermediate scrutiny and every campus in this packet prices or gates it lawfully. NYU, Columbia, Cornell (see the statutory-college wrinkle below), RIT and Fordham are private and owe no public-forum duty at all.

# PART 0C — THE MANHATTAN CLUSTER

**Five of the twelve campuses — NYU, Columbia, Baruch, Hunter and City College — sit within subway distance of one another. This is the densest concentration of target audience in the United States, and nothing else in the fifty-state tour comes close.**

- NYU (Washington Square, 4th St) and Baruch (25th & Lexington) are ~2 miles apart — 15 minutes on the 6.
- Baruch (25th/Lex) and Hunter (68th/Lex) are eight stops on the same 6 line — roughly 12 minutes.
- Hunter (68th/Lex) to Columbia (116th/Broadway) is a 6→shuttle→1 or a single crosstown bus; ~30 minutes.
- Columbia (116th/Broadway) to City College (138th/Convent) is **three stops on the 1 train**, about 6 minutes.

A single ambassador can physically stand on all five campuses in one day. Baruch adds an unusually strong finance audience (Zicklin is CUNY's business school, and its student body is heavily finance- and accounting-track). **If New York is legally viable at all, the Manhattan cluster is the highest-density week available anywhere in the country.** The CUNY three (Baruch, Hunter, CCNY) share a common academic calendar, which means one date works for all three.

## C. Solicitation / outside-vendor policy — ⚠ SPONSORSHIP BY A UNIVERSITY DEPARTMENT IS MANDATORY

**University Event Policy** — https://universitypolicies.columbia.edu/content/university-event-policy
Scope covers "University departments, offices, groups, and student organizations **and non-affiliates** requesting to reserve campus facilities." Operative provisions:

## C. Solicitation / outside-vendor policy — THE INFORMER, 2025-2026

**Tabling — the mechanism DGD would use:**
> "**Your organization may reserve a table in the 2nd Floor Lobby of the Newman Vertical Campus for promotional activities such as the distribution of literature, recruitment drives, ticket sales, etc.**"
> Amplified sound only on Thursdays during club hours via Bluetooth speakers; microphones and bullhorns prohibited.

**⚠⚠ THE PAYMENT-CREDENTIAL CLAUSE — the most directly relevant sentence found anywhere in fifty states:**
> "**Students are not permitted to utilize online ticketing systems or money transfer services when selling tickets. Prohibited services include, but are not limited to, EventBrite, Venmo, Zelle, CashApp, Chime, and other electronic sources of crowdfunding or online money donation platforms.**"
This is written about ticket sales, but the operative words are "**money transfer services**" and "**other electronic sources of crowdfunding or online money donation platforms**." A wallet-funding or credit-purchase flow run at a Baruch table sits squarely inside the mischief this clause addresses. **Raise it before, not after.**

**⚠ THE OUTSIDE-GUEST CAP:**
> "**Only 15% of event participants may be outside guests. Alumni are considered outside guests.**"
Exceptions exist for ticketed shows and case-by-case through the Office of Student Life. A DGD-heavy event would breach this on its face.

**Corporate solicitation:**
> "**Only student members in a registered organization can solicit funds from corporations or other organizations on behalf of a student organization.**"
Clubs must obtain Office of Student Life approval before approaching external companies, except for in-kind gifts; **the Office of College Advancement must also approve corporate solicitation.** So a club that wants DGD sponsorship needs two internal approvals.

**⚠ CONTRACTS — NOBODY SIGNS ANYTHING AT A TABLE:**
> "**All expenditures for personnel services (speakers, musicians, DJs, performers, etc.) require a written contract.**"
> Students must **never** sign contracts; all agreements route through Student Life Advisors via MyBaruch.
> "**Contracts are always signed by the vendor or performer FIRST and then by Baruch, never the other way around.**"
> Contracts must be submitted at least two weeks before the CUNYBuy Purchase Order Request, which is itself due **six to eight weeks prior to the event**. Honoraria cap **$2,000**. Contracts submitted after the performance will not be honoured.

**Fundraising:**
> "**The Office of Student Life must approve all fundraising activities/events (including events that utilize 'suggested donations') in advance.**" Fundraiser Request Form due three weeks ahead. Proceeds must be deposited with the Bursar within one week.
> Raffle prizes and giveaways "**should not exceed $299**"; winners must provide an EMPL number and Baruch ID; members cannot win their own giveaways; approval must precede purchase.

**Off-campus events:** "A student organization event may be held off-campus—regardless of whether student activity fees are being used—**only with the permission and prior approval of the Office of Student Life and the Dean of Students Office**." Eight weeks' notice; six months for international travel; venues must generally carry insurance naming Baruch College as additional insured.

**National affiliation:** "The Office of Student Life reserves the right to request a letter of support from any national or international organization that desires to have a chapter on the Baruch campus."

Above this sits **CUNY Policy 4.02** category 6 ("All other users, including commercial users" — "may permit"), which is the only formal door for DGD as an entity. See Part 0B.

**Procurement:** all purchase requests must "follow the University's established procurement process"; clubs cannot purchase without prior Purchasing Department approval; "**Reimbursements for expenses will not be allowed.**"

**CCNY Event Policy** (separate document) — https://www.ccny.cuny.edu/gca/ccny-event-policy
> External groups must provide insurance documentation meeting CCNY requirements; demonstrate "**significant CCNY interest**" and alignment with the college's "**mission, goals, and ideals**"; comply with all CCNY rules; and have marketing materials approved by the Office of Events Management.
> "**Tickets and marketing materials must clearly identify the sponsoring group, organization or department along with contact information.**"
> **⚠ "No commercial or sales advertising is permitted on campus bulletin boards."**
> **⚠ "Cash transactions are prohibited; only non-cash payment is allowed."**

## (4) The state campus free-speech statute — THERE ISN'T ONE

**New York has NOT enacted a campus free-expression statute for SUNY or CUNY.** SUNY's own 2021 survey of free-expression legislation catalogues statutes in seventeen-plus other states and discusses no New York bill (https://system.suny.edu/sci/news/10-7-21-free-expression-legislation/index.html). A2309 (2023) was introduced (https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/bills/2023/A2309); no enactment found. PEN America's tracker shows the same (https://pen.org/campus-free-speech/the-law-and-campus-free-speech/).

What exists instead is **Article 129-A of the Education Law — the Henderson Act** — which *requires* every New York college, public and private, to adopt and file rules for the maintenance of public order. It is a restriction mandate, not a speech grant. SUNY's implementation is Document 3653 (June 10, 2009): https://www.suny.edu/sunypp/documents.cfm?doc_id=351. CUNY campuses publish theirs individually (e.g. https://sps.cuny.edu/rules-and-regulations-maintenance-public-order-pursuant-article-129a-education-law-henderson-rules · https://studentaffairs.baruch.cuny.edu/dean-of-students/standards/henderson-rules/ · https://www.qcc.cuny.edu/sco/public-order.html).

**⚠ DO NOT LET AN AMBASSADOR CITE A NEW YORK CAMPUS FREE-SPEECH STATUTE. There is none, and the statute that does exist is the one used to eject people.** SUNY and CUNY remain state actors bound by the First Amendment, but commercial speech gets intermediate scrutiny and every fee and approval requirement in this packet is lawful. NYU, Columbia, Cornell, RIT and Fordham are private and owe nothing at all.
