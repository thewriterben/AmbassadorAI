# 0. STATE-LEVEL FINDING (applies to all 9 public campuses)

## Texas Education Code § 51.9315 — "Protected Expression on Campus" (SB 18, 86th Leg., 2019)

Effective **September 1, 2019**. Added by Acts 2019, 86th Leg., R.S., Ch. 568 (S.B. 18).

**Verbatim, § 51.9315(a) definition:**
> "Expressive activities" means "any speech or expressive conduct protected by the First Amendment to the United States Constitution or by Section 8, Article I, Texas Constitution, and includes assemblies, protests, speeches, the distribution of written material, the carrying of signs, and the circulation of petitions."

**Verbatim exclusion — this is the operative fact for DGD:**
> The term does not include "commercial speech; defamation; unlawful harassment; incitement to imminent unlawful activity; obscenity; or threats to engage in unlawful activity."

**§ 51.9315(c)** requires institutions to "permit **students enrolled at and employees of** the institution to engage in expressive activities in the common outdoor areas of the institution's campus freely," if lawful and not "materially and substantially disrupt[ing] the functioning of the institution."

**What this means operationally:**
1. **SB 18 gives DGD nothing.** A crypto project promoting a token/product is commercial speech, which is carved out of the statute by name. The statute's public-forum guarantee runs to *students and employees*, not to outside commercial entities.
2. Every Texas public campus below has built its policy on this carve-out, and several restate it explicitly (UTSA HOP 9.37 flatly prohibits for-profit expressive activity; UNT 07.006 says "Expressive activities do not include commercial speech for purposes of this policy"; UT Austin Institutional Rules 13-205(1)(A) defines commercial speech to include anything bearing a for-profit entity's logo or trademark).
3. Therefore **the viable Texas pathways are contractual, not constitutional**: paid vendor/sponsorship agreements, RSO-sponsored presence where the policy permits it (UH, Texas State), paid tabling at private schools (SMU, Rice), and privately-run hackathon sponsorship.
4. § 51.9315(h): institutions "may not consider any anticipated controversy related to the event" when approving speakers or setting facility fees, and may use only content- and viewpoint-neutral criteria. This is a useful lever if a campus tries to reject DGD *because it's crypto* rather than *because it's commercial* — but the commercial ground is legally solid for them.

> "**Common Outdoor Area**" means outdoor space not used for dedicated University business, educational or research functions on a permanent or temporary basis.

> "Members of the public are able to engage in expressive activities **only in Common Outdoor Areas** of UTSA campuses, subject to the time, place, and manner rules."

> "Members of the public may distribute or display literature in the Common Outside Areas. In either case, **no advanced permission is required.**"

> **THE BAR, verbatim: "Expressive Activities carried out for the purpose of commercial/for-profit benefit (commercial speech) are prohibited."**

> **CO-SPONSORSHIP BAN, verbatim: "No registered student, faculty, or staff organizations … may jointly sponsor any event on campus with an off-campus person or organization."**

**Read carefully — this is a trap for the unwary.** UTSA's literature-distribution rule is genuinely open (no advance permission, members of the public welcome in common outdoor areas). But the very next provision prohibits *commercial/for-profit* expressive activity outright. A DGD ambassador handing out flyers looks permitted right up until the commercial-purpose test is applied, at which point it is flatly prohibited. **Do not read the "no advanced permission is required" sentence in isolation.**

- Orgs must check out tables/chairs from Events Management or bring their own; they cannot repurpose existing Sombrilla or McKinney Building furniture
- Catering must be by an approved caterer

**The one viable UTSA path: a guest-speaker invitation from a student org.** The handbook says in terms that inviting a guest speaker does not constitute prohibited joint sponsorship, and that an org may receive funding from a non-University entity. That is a legitimate, policy-compliant route where tabling is not.

## (4) The state campus free-speech statute

**Texas Education Code § 51.9315, "Protected Expression on Campus"** — added by **SB 18, 86th Legislature, R.S., Ch. 568**, effective **September 1, 2019**. Binds all Texas public institutions of higher education; does **not** bind Rice, Baylor or SMU.

It makes common outdoor areas traditional public forums and abolishes free-speech zones — but **it defines "expressive activities" to exclude commercial speech by name**, and extends its free-access guarantee to "students enrolled at and employees of the institution." A cryptocurrency project marketing a financial product gets no protection from it, and every campus in this set that addresses the point has built its policy on precisely that carve-out.

The one clause that *does* help: **§ 51.9315(h)** — institutions may use only content-neutral and viewpoint-neutral criteria when approving speakers and setting facility fees, and "may not consider any anticipated controversy related to the event." If a campus quotes a higher price or refuses a booking *because crypto is controversial* rather than *because the activity is commercial*, that is a live objection. It will not overcome a straightforward commercial-solicitation denial.

Litigation note: search results surfaced reporting that a separate Texas college-protest law was enjoined for violating free-speech rights ([Texas Policy Research](https://www.texaspolicyresearch.com/texas-college-protest-law-blocked-for-violating-free-speech-rights/)) — **that is a different statute from § 51.9315 and I did not verify its scope.** Do not conflate them.
